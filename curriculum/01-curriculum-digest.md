# Comedy Curriculum Digest 01

Ryan's curriculum, digested. Date: 2026-09-26.

## The thesis that holds it together

Context is king. Ryan's theory: a joke is not a fixed object. It is a transaction between the material, the room, the mood, and the company. His north star is laughing hardest at *This Is the End* in his early 20s, drunk on $2 gas-station shooters dumped into Icees, seated next to two kids way too young to be there. Same film on a Tuesday afternoon, sober and alone, is a different film.

Every item in this curriculum is secretly about this thesis. Byrne says rooms shape music. The *Airplane!* script assumes a packed, disaster-movie-literate room. Improv is the only art form where the room literally writes the show with you. Harmon's circle is a machine for making sure the story survives any room. Read everything below with one question in mind: where does the context do the work?

## 1. Airplane! (1980): the gag machine

Source: full shooting script, June 11 1979 (revised 6/15/79), credited to Jim Abrahams, David Zucker, Jerry Zucker as "Kentucky Fried Theatre's AIRPLANE!" Read in full. Note: a few lines differ from the released film. Quotes below are the script's wording, not the film's.

### Gag density

Nearly every page carries 3 to 6 gags, stacked in three layers: dialogue jokes in the foreground, sight gags in the action lines, running gags paying off in the margins. The film runs about 88 minutes and the script packs roughly 200 distinct jokes into it, about 2 to 3 laughs per minute. Unusually for comedy, the funny is already on the page, in dialogue and parentheticals. This is not a script that asks the actors to find the funny. The actors are asked to protect it by playing everything dead straight.

### Joke taxonomy

**Wordplay and literalization.** The engine of the whole movie. Figures of speech get taken literally or answered literally:
- STRIKER: "Surely you can't be serious." / RUMACK: "I am serious, and don't call me Shirley!"
- MURDOCK: "We have clearance, Clarence." / OVEUR: "Roger, Roger. What's our vector, Victor?"
- OVEUR: "Give me Hamm on five, hold the Mayo." (a call from "a Mister Hamm," while on hold with the Mayo Clinic)

**Deadpan delivery.** Everyone plays the disaster movie straight while the absurd detonates around them. Kramer whips off his sunglasses and underneath is another pair of sunglasses, and nobody reacts. The pilot asks a child, "Have you ever been in a cockpit before?" then, "Have you ever seen a grown man naked?"

**Visual gags.** Written into the action lines, not improvised on set: the tail fin emerging through clouds like a shark's fin; the "automatic pilot" as an instantly inflatable balloon pilot that later deflates; the St. Christopher statue holding a motion-discomfort bag to its mouth; a spear slamming into the wall behind Kramer mid-speech; a watermelon falling from the top of the frame and splattering.

**Running gags.** Set up early, paid off repeatedly, escalating each time:
- The white-zone/red-zone PA announcement escalates from a loading-zone message into a domestic fight ending in gunshots.
- McCroskey's "Looks like I picked the wrong week to quit smoking," then drinking (while swigging), then amphetamines (while popping pills), then sniffing glue (while sniffing airplane glue).
- Ted's "drinking problem" is literalized: he pours Gator-Ade on his forehead, pours a drink between his cheek and ear, pours fountain water on his head.

**Background gags.** Jokes that never stop the scene: Mrs. Hammen's thought bubbles ("Jim never has a second cup of coffee at home"). An Air Israel plane with a beard, yarmulka, and tallis. The businessman still waiting in Ted's cab as the meter climbs to $389.10.

**Parody.** The skeleton is *Zero Hour!* (1957), beat for beat: traumatized ex-pilot, food poisoning, must land the plane, coached from the tower. Layered spoofs: *Jaws* in the opening, *Saturday Night Fever* in the disco dance, "Win one for the Gipper" in Rumack's "win just one for the Zipper" speech, Ethel Merman musicals in shell-shocked Lt. Hurwitz singing "Everything's Coming Up Roses."

### Structure

Cold open (Jaws parody, then the airport terminal as a gag-delivery machine). Setup (boarding introduces the ensemble; flashbacks dramatize Ted and Elaine's romance as war-movie parody). Escalation (food poisoning chain reaction, pilots drop one by one, Ted forced into the cockpit; every disaster beat is a straight scene that gets punctured). Climax (the landing, intercut with the inflight movie of crashing planes). Button (the autopilot salutes and winks at the camera).

How the parody serves the gag machine: the disaster plot is a seriousness conveyor belt. Every beat is played with total gravity, which is what makes the literalized punchlines land. The ticking clock of poisoned passengers means new gags can bolt onto any scene without slowing anything down. And because the plot mirrors *Zero Hour!*, the disaster-movie-literate audience always knows what beat is coming, so the script spends all its energy on the swerve instead of the setup.

### Three stealable techniques

**1. Answer the idiom literally.** Write the normal line first, then ask what it looks like if the character meant it physically. For sketch work: scan a draft for idioms and stage one of them. That single pass surfaces jokes you did not know were in the script.

**2. Let the straight scene absorb the absurdity.** The disaster plot never winks. Kramer delivers a grave monologue while a spear slams into the wall behind him, and the scene continues without a blink. For sketch work: keep the stakes real and the tone sober, and let the joke be the one thing in the room that refuses to acknowledge the tone. The contrast is the laugh.

**3. Escalate a runner by changing its meaning.** The white-zone PA starts as a functional airport message and becomes a domestic crisis ending in gunshots. Each return keeps the original shape but raises the absurdity. For sketch work: never just repeat a catchphrase. Bring the bit back in a new context where the same words mean something more desperate or more wrong than before.

### Context note

This script assumes a packed, knowing room. The parody only pays off if the audience recognizes the disaster-movie beats being spoofed. The same script in a room that never saw *Zero Hour!* loses a whole layer. That is the thesis, demonstrated: the room is a co-author.

Flag: the jive-talk translation gag is in the script and is dated material. Worth noting if the skill ever discusses it, and a reminder that context includes era.

## 2. Longform improv: ASSSSCAT, the Harold, and the four schools

### ASSSSCAT

UCB's flagship and longest-running show. Spelled with four S's. Format: an audience suggestion prompts a guest monologist to share off-the-cuff stories and feelings; the improvisers use the monologue as fodder for improvised scenes. Two halves per show, each with its own suggestion and monologue. It grew out of the *Armando Diaz Theatrical Experience and Hootenanny* in Chicago, came east when Poehler, Besser, Walsh, and Roberts moved to New York in 1996, and went bicoastal with UCB LA in 2005. The Village Voice called it the world's longest-running improv show; Vice called it the gold standard. Traditionally Sunday nights: an early ticketed show and a free late show.

Why it matters here: ASSSSCAT is context-is-king made literal. The room gives the suggestion, a stranger gives the raw material, and the show cannot exist without either.

### The Harold

Del Close's canonical longform structure (Close, 1941-1999: Chicago improv teacher, Second City and iO director, godfather of modern improvisation). The form traces to San Francisco's The Committee, who named it after a line in *A Hard Day's Night* where George Harrison calls his haircut "Arthur." Close refined it into the teachable form, codified with Charna Halpern at ImprovOlympic (iO). The UCB founders trained under him there.

The typical Harold, per the *UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual*:

- **Suggestion and opening.** One audience suggestion, then the ensemble explores it in an opening: rotating monologues, the invocation (poetic choral free-association), or a pattern game (word association and riffing).
- **First beat.** Three unconnected two-person scenes (A1, B1, C1), each launching its own comic game or pattern.
- **Group game.** The whole cast together, mining the material.
- **Second beat.** Revisit each scene (A2, B2, C2), heightening the games. Scenes stay disconnected.
- **Group game.** Again, all cast.
- **Third beat.** Shorter scenes that heighten further and let the worlds collide, ideally collapsing into one scene that folds all three games together.

Close called it a 3x3 structure but insisted the format belonged to the performers; departures were encouraged. His maxim: "the first rule is: there are no rules." Variants include the Armando (Harold with a guest monologist telling true stories), the Sybil (solo), and the Monoscene.

Why it matters here: the Harold is a machine for manufacturing connection out of chaos. Three unrelated threads, and the form promises they will collide. That promise, kept or broken in front of a live room, is the whole thrill. Compare to the Airplane! runner technique: same move, different medium. Plant unrelated, converge late, heighten each time.

### The four schools, one line each

- **UCB (Upright Citizens Brigade):** Founded 1996 in New York by Matt Besser, Amy Poehler, Ian Roberts, and Matt Walsh, Chicago-trained under Del Close at iO; known for grounded-then-absurd longform, the game-of-the-scene style, and as the dominant talent pipeline to SNL and TV comedy of the 2000s.
- **Second City:** Founded 1959 in Chicago by Bernie Sahlins, Paul Sills, and Howard Alk; known for scripted sketch revues developed from improv, and as the historic feeder of SNL's earliest casts (Belushi, Aykroyd, Murray, Ramis, Fey, Carell, Colbert).
- **iO (formerly ImprovOlympic):** Founded 1981 in Chicago by Del Close and Charna Halpern; known as the home and training ground of the Harold and the birthplace of modern longform.
- **The Groundlings:** Founded 1974 in Los Angeles by Gary Austin; known for character-based sketch and improv, the showcase-to-main-company system, and alumni like Paul Reubens (who built Pee-wee Herman there with Phil Hartman), Will Ferrell, Kristen Wiig, and Maya Rudolph.

## 3. Dan Harmon's story circle

The 8 steps, from Harmon's own "Story Structure 104: The Juicy Details":

1. **You** (a character is in a zone of comfort)
2. **Need** (but they want something)
3. **Go** (they enter an unfamiliar situation)
4. **Search** (adapt to it)
5. **Find** (find what they wanted)
6. **Take** (pay its price)
7. **Return** (and go back to where they started)
8. **Change** (now capable of change)

Harmon's own framing: the real structure of any good story is "simply circular: a descent into the unknown and eventual return." Top half of the circle is the ordinary world, bottom half the special world; steps 1 through 4 are descent, 5 through 8 are ascent.

**Origin.** Harmon started on it in the late 1990s while stuck on a screenplay, chasing what he called "some symmetry to this. Some simplicity." The practical trigger was Channel 101, the monthly short-film festival he ran: directors said they could not write plots for television, so he simplified Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey (*The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949) into an eight-step circle that reliably produced coherent stories. He published it as "Story Structure 101: Super Basic Shit" and "Story Structure 104: The Juicy Details" on his Channel 101 site. On Campbell, in his own words: "Joseph Campbell was a comparative mythologist, not a corny screenwriting guru. Nevertheless, here is where I, Dan Harmon, feel that the chapters of Campbell's famous 'monomyth' or 'hero's journey' would fall if you forced them into my circle." He has also said, "I can't not see that circle. It's tattooed on my brain."

**Why a structure nerd loves it.** It is fractal: a pilot can run steps 1 through 3 while the whole series forms a larger circle, yet each episode still runs a full one. *Community* episodes routinely send multiple characters through their own circles inside the group's bigger one. It is a testable loop: audit a draft against the eight steps and name the missing one. The canonical demo is the critique of *Community* S4E1, which felt off because no character completed a full circle. The most commonly missing step in broken stories is 6 (Take): the character gets what they wanted at no cost, so nothing was at stake. And it ran real rooms: Harmon built it to teach Channel 101 directors, then used it on *Community* and *Rick and Morty*. The meta layer is on screen too: Abed references the circle's stages in the *Community* season 5 finale, and *Rick and Morty*'s "Never Ricking Morty" literally draws the circle and name-checks "paying a heavy price."

**Context note.** Step 8 is where the thesis lives. The character returns to the same room, changed. The room did not change; the person in it did. Every good story is a context experiment with a sample size of one.

## 5. The Byrne link (already in the queue)

No re-research needed; just the connection. David Byrne's *How Music Works* argues that music is shaped by the rooms and venues it is made for: music composed for cathedrals, for small clubs, for outdoor fields, for headphones. The architecture comes first and the art adapts to fit it. Ryan assigned Byrne first because the context argument lands hardest in music, where nobody disputes it, and then it ports cleanly to comedy: the room shapes the scene. Byrne is the thesis in its most defensible form. Everything after him is the same argument wearing a rubber nose.

## Three candidate first sketches

Each one exercises a curriculum item. Each one is mine: dry, absurd, a little unhinged, old-scraggly little-guy chaos with a warm underbelly.

**1. "We're a Family Here."** A job interview. The interviewer says "we're a family here," and the candidate takes it literally: starts calling everyone Mom and Dad, asks who's hosting Thanksgiving, wants to know the Wi-Fi password for "home." The interviewer tries to walk it back; every corporate platitude gets literalized in turn ("open-door policy" means he removes the office door; "we wear many hats" ends with a hat rack). Technique: Airplane! rule 1, answer the idiom literally, escalated by rule 3, each return more desperate. The warm underbelly: by the end, the office is weirdly fond of him, and the interviewer almost means it about the family thing. Almost.

**2. "The Retired Gags Support Group."** A church basement, folding chairs, weak coffee. Old catchphrases from dead sitcoms and retired running gags meet to talk about life after the laugh track. One of them still gets quoted at parties and feels guilty about it. One of them was never funny, just repeated, and has made peace with that. They do the thing where they try not to say their catchphrase and then all say it at once. Technique: the Harold's third beat, three separate sad little stories colliding into one shared scene. The warm underbelly is the whole sketch: they still make each other laugh, and that is the only audience that ever mattered.

**3. "Same Set, Three Rooms."** A comedian performs the exact same five-minute set three times: a packed club at midnight, a corporate lunch, and a children's birthday party. The set never changes a word. The laughs move anyway. At the birthday party, two kids are way too young to be there, and they laugh hardest at the bit about mortality. Technique: the thesis itself, staged as an experiment. The warm underbelly: the comic bombs at the corporate lunch, kills at midnight, and at the birthday party a kid asks for the joke again, slower, and it becomes the best version of all.
